Abstract
Urban Heat Island (UHI) effects are becoming increasingly important in rapidly urbanising cities, where changes in land cover, vegetation, surface properties, topography, and atmospheric conditions contribute to substantial spatial and temporal variations in urban heat. Metropolitan Mumbai presents a particularly complex environment due to its dense built-up structure, coastal setting, heterogeneous land cover, and continuing urban development.
This study presents an AI-driven spatiotemporal framework for modelling and downscaling urban heat island dynamics across Metropolitan Mumbai. The framework integrates multi-source satellite observations from Landsat 8/9 and Sentinel-2 with Shuttle Radar Topography Mission elevation data and long-term meteorological observations from the India Meteorological Department. Remote sensing and environmental variables are combined to construct a machine learning predictor space for high-resolution land surface temperature estimation. XGBoost and Random Forest models are evaluated using spatial block cross-validation to provide a more reliable assessment of model generalisation. Explainable artificial intelligence using SHAP is subsequently employed to quantify the contribution of individual predictors to the predicted thermal patterns.
The framework further incorporates long-term temperature trend analysis and scenario-based simulation to evaluate potential urban cooling interventions. The resulting system provides spatially explicit heat estimates, identifies important environmental drivers of urban thermal variability, and supports assessment of alternative mitigation scenarios. The proposed approach demonstrates how multi-source remote sensing, meteorological observations, machine learning, spatially aware validation, and explainable modelling can be integrated into a unified framework for evidence-based urban heat assessment and climate-resilient planning in Mumbai.

1	Introduction
Urban Heat Island (UHI) is a major manifestation of urban climate change in which urbanised areas experience higher temperatures than their less-developed surroundings. It results from interactions among land cover transformation, surface materials, vegetation, urban morphology, atmospheric conditions, and anthropogenic activities. The conversion of natural surfaces into impervious materials such as concrete and asphalt alters the surface energy balance, while reduced vegetation limits natural cooling through evapotranspiration. Consequently, urban thermal conditions can vary substantially across densely built-up areas, vegetated regions, open land, and water bodies. Reliable spatial assessment of urban heat is therefore important for sustainable urban planning and climate resilience.
The challenge is particularly relevant to coastal megacities such as Mumbai, where dense development occurs alongside vegetation, open spaces, coastal zones, and water bodies. These heterogeneous environments produce complex spatial patterns of land surface temperature (LST). Remote sensing provides an effective means of characterising these patterns through satellite-derived LST and complementary indicators such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI), and Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI). Additional variables including albedo, emissivity, elevation, slope, aspect, and atmospheric conditions can further describe the processes influencing urban thermal behaviour.
Recent studies have increasingly applied machine learning to LST and UHI modelling because urban thermal relationships are nonlinear and involve interactions between multiple environmental variables. Ensemble approaches such as Random Forest and gradient-boosting methods can model these relationships using multiple spatial and atmospheric predictors. However, model evaluation presents an important challenge in spatial environmental studies. Random train-test partitioning can place spatially neighbouring observations in both training and testing datasets, resulting in spatial information leakage and potentially optimistic estimates of model performance. Spatially independent validation is therefore necessary to assess whether a model can generalise to locations not represented during training.
Another limitation of conventional UHI studies is the separation of long-term climate analysis, spatial prediction, model interpretation, and mitigation assessment. Long-term meteorological observations provide information about temporal temperature changes, whereas satellite observations provide detailed spatial information. Similarly, identifying thermal hotspots alone does not indicate which environmental variables are most influential or how potential urban interventions could modify thermal conditions. Integrating these components within a common framework can therefore provide a more comprehensive assessment of urban heat dynamics.
To address these challenges, this study develops an AI-driven spatiotemporal framework for modelling and downscaling urban heat across Metropolitan Mumbai. The framework integrates satellite observations acquired through Google Earth Engine with long-term meteorological observations. Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2, and SRTM data are combined with meteorological variables to construct a 17-feature predictor matrix consisting of surface, vegetation, built-up, topographic, spatial, and atmospheric characteristics. XGBoost and Random Forest models are evaluated for LST prediction, with spatial block cross-validation incorporating a spatial exclusion buffer to reduce the influence of spatial autocorrelation.
The framework further incorporates explainable artificial intelligence using TreeSHAP to examine the contribution of individual predictors to the modelled thermal conditions. Long-term temperature trends are analysed using non-parametric trend analysis, while an intervention-oriented simulation component evaluates hypothetical changes in selected urban characteristics, including vegetation, surface albedo, and built-up intensity. This connects spatial prediction and model interpretation with potential urban cooling strategies.
The main objectives of the study are to develop a quality-controlled multi-source dataset for urban thermal analysis; model spatial LST using machine learning evaluate model generalisation using spatially independent validation; identify important thermal drivers using explainable machine learning; characterise long-term temperature trends using multi-decadal meteorological observations; and evaluate potential urban cooling interventions through scenario-based simulation. The resulting framework integrates remote sensing, meteorological observations, machine learning, spatial validation, explainable artificial intelligence, and simulation within a unified approach for analysing urban thermal dynamics.
2	Study Area
The study was conducted across the municipal area of Mumbai, Maharashtra, India, covering the administrative jurisdiction represented by the 24 official municipal wards of the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC). The study domain covers approximately 437.71 km and represents a highly heterogeneous urban environment consisting of densely built-up neighbourhoods, residential and commercial areas, transportation infrastructure, vegetation, open spaces, coastal regions, and water bodies. This spatial heterogeneity makes Mumbai an appropriate study area for investigating the distribution and controlling factors of urban heat.
Mumbai is a coastal metropolitan environment in western India, where the interaction between dense urban development and the surrounding Arabian Sea produces a complex thermal setting. The city contains substantial variations in surface characteristics over relatively short distances, including highly impervious built-up areas, vegetated zones, open land, and coastal surfaces. Such variations influence the absorption, storage, and release of energy at the land surface and contribute to spatial differences in land surface temperature.
The urban structure of Mumbai has undergone substantial development, resulting in considerable variation in building density, vegetation availability, surface materials, and land-use characteristics between different parts of the city. These variations are particularly relevant to machine-learning-based thermal modelling because they provide a broad range of environmental conditions from which relationships between land surface temperature and its explanatory variables can be learned.
The study boundary was defined using the official BMC ward geometry and was maintained consistently across the spatial processing workflow. All satellite-derived variables, topographic characteristics, and spatial predictors were clipped or associated with this common study domain before integration with the meteorological observations. The spatial datasets were processed using the Universal Transverse Mercator coordinate reference system, Zone 43 North (EPSG:32643), providing a consistent projected coordinate system for spatial analysis and distance-based calculations.
The study area was selected to support an integrated analysis of both the spatial and temporal dimensions of urban heat. Satellite observations provide spatially distributed information on surface temperature, vegetation, built-up characteristics, water presence, surface properties, and topography, while long-term meteorological observations provide the temporal context required to examine changes in thermal conditions. The combination of these datasets enables the developed framework to examine urban heat across heterogeneous municipal environments while maintaining a common spatial reference for subsequent machine-learning and spatial validation analyses.

3	Methods
The proposed methodology integrates multi-source remote sensing, long-term meteorological observations, machine learning, spatially independent validation, explainable artificial intelligence, statistical trend analysis, and intervention-based simulation to investigate the spatial and temporal characteristics of urban heat across metropolitan Mumbai. The framework is designed to combine high-resolution satellite-derived surface information with long-term meteorological observations so that both spatial variability and temporal behaviour of urban thermal conditions can be examined within a common modelling framework. The overall workflow consists of satellite data acquisition and preprocessing, land surface temperature and biophysical feature generation, meteorological data quality control and feature derivation, integration of multi-source predictors, machine learning-based LST modelling, spatially independent validation, model interpretation using SHAP, long-term temperature trend analysis, and scenario-based thermal intervention simulation. Satellite observations from Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-2, and SRTM were processed within Google Earth Engine (GEE), while the long-term meteorological dataset was processed separately to identify and correct anomalous observations, reconstruct short temporal gaps, and derive additional atmospheric variables. The resulting datasets were synchronised according to the satellite observation period and integrated into a 17-feature predictor matrix. XGBoost and Random Forest models were subsequently trained to estimate LST from the combined environmental and meteorological predictors. To reduce the effect of spatial dependence between training and testing observations, spatially constrained validation was performed using a 1.2 km exclusion buffer. TreeSHAP was then used to interpret the contribution and direction of individual predictors to the modelled thermal response. In addition, the long-term meteorological record was analysed using non-parametric trend analysis, while the trained model was used to simulate hypothetical changes in vegetation, surface reflectivity, and built-up intensity and evaluate their potential influence on urban thermal conditions.
3.1	Overall methodological framework
The proposed framework establishes an end-to-end workflow for modelling urban thermal variability across Mumbai by integrating spatial remote sensing observations with long-term atmospheric measurements and machine learning. The methodology begins with the acquisition of satellite observations within the official Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation (BMC) study boundary, followed by quality filtering, spatial preprocessing, and extraction of thermal, spectral, biophysical, and topographic information. Landsat 8/9 observations provide the primary thermal and multispectral information, while Sentinel-2 observations provide complementary optical information and SRTM provides elevation-derived terrain characteristics. The satellite processing stage produces land surface temperature together with variables representing vegetation, built-up surfaces, water, surface reflectivity, vegetation fraction, emissivity, topography, and spatial location. In parallel, long-term meteorological observations covering 1997–2024 are subjected to quality control and temporal preprocessing, including robust outlier detection, short-gap interpolation, wind-vector decomposition, and derivation of vapour pressure deficit. The meteorological observations corresponding to the satellite acquisition period are then synchronised with the satellite-derived observations. These datasets are integrated to form a 17-feature predictor matrix representing surface, atmospheric, topographic, and geographic controls on LST. Two tree-based machine learning approaches, XGBoost and Random Forest, are used to model the nonlinear relationship between these predictors and observed LST. Model performance is assessed using conventional evaluation as well as spatially independent validation based on spatial blocks and a 1.2 km exclusion buffer to minimise spatial leakage. The resulting model is interpreted using TreeSHAP to identify the relative contribution and direction of the predictors. Alongside the spatial modelling framework, the long-term meteorological record is analysed using Mann–Kendall trend analysis and Sen’s slope estimation to quantify temporal temperature changes. Finally, the trained model is incorporated into a scenario-based simulation framework in which changes in vegetation, albedo, and built-up intensity can be introduced to estimate their potential thermal effects. This integrated workflow therefore connects satellite-based spatial characterisation, meteorological temporal analysis, machine learning-based downscaling, spatial validation, model interpretation, and intervention assessment within a single methodological framework.
3.2	Satellite data acquisition and processing
Satellite observations were used to characterise the spatial variability of surface thermal and biophysical conditions across the study area. Data acquisition and initial processing were performed using Google Earth Engine (GEE), allowing the required satellite observations to be spatially filtered using the BMC study boundary and processed within a consistent geospatial environment. Landsat 8 and Landsat 9 observations were used as the primary source of thermal and multispectral information. The analysis focused on the pre-monsoon period from 1 February to 31 May 2024, during which 14 suitable satellite scenes were processed. Automated quality screening was applied using the quality-assurance information associated with the Landsat observations to remove pixels affected by cloud and cloud-shadow contamination. Sentinel-2 multispectral observations were incorporated to provide complementary optical information for characterising vegetation, built-up surfaces, water features, and surface properties. SRTM digital elevation data were used to derive topographic characteristics across the study area. All spatial datasets were clipped to the common study boundary and transformed to the project coordinate reference system, EPSG:32643, before their integration into the modelling dataset. The processed satellite observations were subsequently sampled to generate the spatial observations used for model development and validation.
3.3	Land surface temperature and biophysical feature generation
The satellite observations were transformed into a set of surface and environmental predictors representing the major factors associated with urban thermal variability. The resulting variables describe vegetation condition, built-up intensity, surface water, surface radiative properties, topography, and spatial position. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was derived to represent vegetation abundance, while the Normalized Difference Built-up Index (NDBI) was used to characterise the relative presence of built-up and impervious surfaces. The Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) was calculated to represent surface-water characteristics, while surface albedo was incorporated to describe the reflective properties of the land surface. Fractional vegetation cover () was derived from vegetation information to provide a continuous representation of vegetation fraction, and surface emissivity () was incorporated to account for differences in the thermal emission characteristics of surface materials. SRTM elevation data were used to derive elevation, slope, and aspect, providing information on topographic controls on surface temperature. Distance to the coastline together with latitude and longitude were included to represent spatial and coastal gradients across Mumbai. The satellite-derived variables were spatially associated with the corresponding LST observations to construct the response and predictor dataset used for subsequent machine learning analysis. This process produced a consistent set of spatially referenced surface, topographic, and geographic variables that could subsequently be integrated with the meteorological observations.

3.4	Meteorological data processing
Long-term meteorological observations were incorporated to represent the temporal atmospheric conditions associated with urban thermal variability. The meteorological dataset contains 383,640 observations covering the period from 1997 to 2024. Quality control was performed before feature integration to identify anomalous observations and minimise their influence on subsequent analysis. A robust Hampel filtering procedure based on the median absolute deviation (MAD) was applied for outlier detection, while short temporal gaps of up to 3 h were reconstructed using Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP) interpolation. 3.5	Multi-source feature integration
The processed satellite and meteorological datasets were integrated to construct a unified predictor matrix for machine learning-based LST modelling. A total of 17 predictor variables were selected to represent complementary surface, atmospheric, topographic, and geographic controls on urban thermal variability. The predictor set consisted of NDVI, NDBI, MNDWI, albedo, fractional vegetation cover (), surface emissivity (), elevation, slope, aspect, distance to the coastline, latitude, longitude, dry-bulb temperature (), relative humidity (RH), zonal wind component (), meridional wind component (), and vapour pressure deficit (VPD). Land surface temperature was retained as the response variable. The integration process spatially associated the satellite-derived variables with their corresponding observations and temporally matched the atmospheric variables with the satellite acquisition period. This produced a consistent modelling dataset in which each observation contained the required surface, atmospheric, topographic, and geographic information for estimating LST. The resulting multi-source representation was used as the common input dataset for subsequent machine learning, validation, interpretation, and intervention-simulation stages.
3.6	Machine learning-based LST downscaling
Machine learning was employed to model the nonlinear relationship between the integrated environmental predictors and observed land surface temperature. Two tree-based ensemble algorithms, XGBoost and Random Forest, were implemented for the modelling task. XGBoost was used as the primary modelling approach because of its ability to represent nonlinear interactions between urban surface characteristics, atmospheric conditions, and spatial variables through gradient-boosted decision trees. Random Forest was implemented as a comparative ensemble model based on multiple decision trees and randomised feature and sample selection. The models were trained using the integrated predictor matrix with observed LST as the target variable. Model performance was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (). Both conventional random validation and spatially constrained validation were considered to distinguish predictive performance under randomly sampled observations from performance when spatial dependence between training and testing observations is explicitly controlled. The resulting models provided the basis for spatial LST estimation, feature interpretation, and subsequent intervention-based simulation.
3.7	Spatially independent model validation
Spatial dependence between neighbouring observations can lead to overly optimistic estimates of machine learning performance when conventional random train–test splitting is used. To address this issue, spatially constrained validation was incorporated into the modelling framework using SpatialBlockKFold validation. A 1.2 km spatial exclusion buffer was applied between training and validation observations so that observations located within the specified neighbourhood of the validation samples were excluded from the corresponding training set. The exclusion distance was selected based on the spatial dependence characteristics identified during the analysis and was used to reduce the influence of spatial autocorrelation and spatial information leakage. The spatial validation procedure therefore evaluates the ability of the model to generalise beyond closely neighbouring observations rather than only measuring its ability to interpolate within areas represented in the training data. Model performance obtained from spatial validation was compared with conventional random validation using RMSE, MAE, and , providing a more conservative assessment of the spatial generalisation capability of the LST downscaling model.

3.8	Explainable machine learning using SHAP
Explainable machine learning was incorporated to investigate how individual predictors influenced the LST predictions generated by the trained tree-based models. TreeSHAP was used to calculate feature-level Shapley values for the XGBoost model, providing an additive representation of the contribution of individual predictors to each prediction relative to the model baseline. The resulting SHAP values were used to determine both the relative importance and direction of predictor contributions. Positive SHAP values indicate that a feature contributes towards increasing the predicted LST relative to the model baseline, whereas negative values indicate a contribution towards lower predicted LST. The resulting feature-level explanations were used to identify the dominant environmental, surface, atmospheric, and spatial controls represented within the model. This interpretation provides an additional layer of analysis beyond predictive accuracy by connecting the modelled thermal response with the physical characteristics represented by the predictor variables.

3.9	Long-term temperature trend analysis
Long-term temperature variability was examined using the meteorological observations covering the period from 1997 to 2024. The Mann–Kendall non-parametric trend test was applied to determine whether a statistically significant monotonic trend was present in the temperature record. The corresponding standardized test statistic was used to determine the direction and significance of the observed trend. Sen’s slope estimator was subsequently applied to quantify the magnitude of the temporal change by estimating the median slope across all possible observation pairs. 7	Cross-references
In electronic publications, articles may be internally hyperlinked. Hyperlinks are generated from proper cross-references in the article. For example, the words Fig. 1 will never be more than simple text, whereas the proper cross-reference \ref{tiger} may be turned into a hyperlink to the figure itself: Fig. 1. In the same way, the words Ref. [1] will fail to turn into a hyperlink; the proper cross-reference is \cite{Knuth96}. Cross-referencing is possible in LaTeX for sections, subsections, formulae, figures, tables, and literature references.
